How to read this atlas

Five questions. No single score.

A book may closely reproduce Hebrew word order while choosing flexible vocabulary. It may read naturally in Greek and still follow its source. Keep the questions separate.

01

Words

Does one source word regularly receive the same Greek equivalent? Lexical consistency is only one kind of closeness.

02

Structure

How much source word order, grammar, and sentence structure survives in Greek?

03

Extent

What is added, omitted, condensed, or rearranged? A difference may belong to the source manuscript.

04

Idiom

Are Hebrew expressions carried across, interpreted, or replaced by Greek ways of speaking?

05

Readability

How does the Greek work as Greek? Smoothness does not by itself tell us how closely it translates.

A guide to tendencies, not a league table.

The spectrum categories are this guide’s teaching synthesis of the named introductions. They are editorial categories, not a classification used by NETS or its contributors. “Mixed” means several tendencies need to be held together. “Unranked” means translation comparison is inappropriate or insufficiently secure. Neither is a midpoint score.

We use the Greek text discussed by each NETS contributor, generally a critical reconstruction. A description does not cover every manuscript, verse, later revision, or modern English translation. Read the scope and caution on each profile.

A difference can begin before translation.

The Masoretic Text is a major Hebrew comparison text, but it is not necessarily identical to the source a translator used. A shorter Greek book does not automatically prove that its translator deleted material.

Daniel, Tobit, Judges, and Esther survive in more than one Greek form and need particular attention to versions. Samuel and Kings change character where a later revision takes over.

Key terms

Vorlage
The source text in front of a translator. It may differ from any Hebrew manuscript we now possess.
MT (Masoretic Text)
The medieval Hebrew text tradition used as the standard point of comparison.
Old Greek (OG)
The earliest recoverable Greek translation of a book, as distinct from later revisions.
Kaige
A revisional tendency that brought existing Greek closer to a Hebrew text like the later MT. Named for its habit of rendering Hebrew gam with καί γε.
Theodotion
A label for certain Greek text forms, notably in Daniel and Job. The label does not by itself date or attribute them.
NETS
A New English Translation of the Septuagint (Oxford, 2007). Its book introductions are the main evidence base here.

Terminology follows the NETS introductions; for kaige, see Satterthwaite, NETS Judges, printed p. 195. The five questions above are this atlas’s teaching framework.

Dating the Greek text

Which event are we dating?

Composition is the writing of a work; translation is its rendering into Greek; revision is the reworking of an existing Greek text. For Greek originals, the displayed date is composition. A date for the Hebrew or Aramaic source is labelled separately when included.

Ranges record named scholarly proposals, not measured confidence intervals or a consensus on every book. “Unknown” means the evidence consulted here does not justify a secure date; “disputed” signals competing proposals. A manuscript or quotation can supply a latest possible boundary, not an exact translation year. Later manuscripts can preserve earlier Greek.

Timeline bars are schematic displays of the cited proposals, not exact historical boundaries. A bar may span alternative dates or depict an approximate century; its endpoints add no evidence beyond the profile’s wording. Proposals without usable numerical boundaries are left without bars. Open the profile for the actual date claim and its source.

Dating notes researched 12 November 2026. Several grouped profiles contain more than one historical layer; their dates should not be applied uniformly to every verse or manuscript. Follow the date-specific citations as well as the translation-profile sources.

Evidence, uncertainty, and the limits of this edition

The core evidence is the book introductions to A New English Translation of the Septuagint (NETS), edited by Albert Pietersma and Benjamin G. Wright (Oxford University Press, 2007; online files include corrections). Each full profile identifies its sources with section or page locators; cards are abbreviated views of those profiles. “Direct comparison” means an extant Hebrew or Aramaic comparator is available; it does not mean we possess the translator’s exact manuscript. “Qualified comparison” marks especially significant version or source issues. “Inferred source” marks a missing or disputed Semitic original.

NETS is an English translation with a stated interpretive model; its English surface is not a transparent measurement of ancient Greek. Its interlinear paradigm is a scholarly approach, not an undisputed account of every book’s original purpose. Marieke Dhont’s study of stylistic diversity cautions against reducing translation to one axis or treating source interference as simply “bad Greek.” Read Dhont, especially pp. 1–8 ›

This edition groups Chronicles, Ezra–Nehemiah, and the Twelve where the cited introduction treats them together. The Twelve’s group profile is not an individual ranking of all twelve books. Psalm 151, the Prayer of Manasseh, Odes, Psalms of Solomon, and the Prayer of Azariah / Song of the Three do not yet have separate profiles. Susanna and Bel and the Dragon have joint-version profiles. All groupings need passage-level checking before use in an argument. Profile descriptions and passage discussions paraphrase the named sources; the teaching categories and five-question framework are editorial synthesis. Greek excerpts are quotations, identified by book, chapter, verse, and version. Their base edition is Alfred Rahlfs’s 1935 Septuaginta, not the 2006 Rahlfs–Hanhart revision. Brief English glosses explain the Greek and should not be cited as verbatim NETS translations. See text and supplementary references. Consult the relevant critical edition for textual variants.
