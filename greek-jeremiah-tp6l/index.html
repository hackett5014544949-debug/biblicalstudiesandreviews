Foundations of the Septuagint · Module 4

How the Greek Old Testament changed over time

Student guide · Chapter 4 and a worked example: Greek Jeremiah

Part 1 · From Lanier and Ross, Chapter 4

How did the Greek Old Testament develop?

It did not arrive as one fixed text and stay that way. It was copied, corrected, retranslated and compared for centuries.

If a text is copied by hand for centuries, what happens to it?
Over the centuries

Hand copying

Jewish and Christian scribes copied the Greek Old Testament by hand.

The result

Variants

Copying produced both accidental and deliberate differences. Scholars sort them to get back toward the earliest wording.

A working term

Old Greek

The earliest Greek translation, as distinct from the revisions made to it later.

The plan for today. Three layers of change that Lanier and Ross describe, then one book where we can test them.

Part 2 · Layer one

Revision: pulling the Greek toward the Hebrew

Over time, people revised the Greek translations, and the usual direction was toward the Hebrew text they knew.

Revisers often worked to “bring them closer to the Hebrew text.”

Greg Lanier and William A. Ross, “7 Questions about the Septuagint,” Crossway. Quotation is word for word; the sentence around it is a paraphrase.
Around the turn of the era

Kaige

A revising tradition named for its habit of rendering a Hebrew word for “also” or “even” as καίγε. Best known from a Greek Minor Prophets scroll found at Nahal Hever.

Why it matters

Layers

Each revision sits on top of the older Greek. Scholars work backward through the layers to reach the Old Greek.

Part 3 · Layer two

Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion

In the second century, three more Greek versions of the Hebrew Scriptures appeared, each with its own instinct.

Second century AD

Aquila

Extremely literal. Famous for matching the Hebrew word for word, even where the Greek turns awkward.

Second century AD

Symmachus

The opposite instinct: clear, idiomatic Greek that aims at the sense rather than the word order.

Second century AD

Theodotion

Stays close to the older Greek while correcting it toward the Hebrew. His Daniel replaced the Old Greek in church use.

Recap from the lecture, Isaiah 7:14. The older Greek reads παρθένος, “virgin.” The three later versions are reported to read νεᾶνις, “young woman.” The difference comes from a revising choice, not from a different Hebrew edition.

Part 4 · Layer three

Origen’s Hexapla

In the 230s AD, Origen laid six versions side by side at Caesarea so that readers could see exactly where Greek and Hebrew differed.

2Hebrew in Greek letters

※Asterisk

Marks material found in the Hebrew but missing from the Greek. Origen filled these gaps mainly from Theodotion.

÷Obelus

Marks material found in the Greek but missing from the Hebrew.

A later Antiochene, or Lucianic, recension also exists. Its background is still debated.

Part 5 · From the book to Jeremiah

A harder kind of change

Revision, new versions and Origen all happened after the Greek existed. Greek Jeremiah raises a different question.

What the book showed us

Changes made to the Greek

Someone copied it, revised it, retranslated it, or compared it with the Hebrew.

What Jeremiah asks

A difference already in the Hebrew

Did the Greek differ because the Hebrew behind it already differed, before anyone translated?

How would you tell a change made to the Greek from a difference that was already in the Hebrew?

Part 6 · Opening question

Where do you find the Oracles Against the Nations?

Open your Bible to Jeremiah. Where are the oracles against Egypt, Moab, Edom and Babylon?

In English Bibles they sit near the end of the book, in chapters 46 to 51. In the Greek Old Testament they sit in the middle, right after 25:13. Same prophet, same oracles, different arrangement.

Today's question: when two ancient texts of one book disagree, did someone change the text after it was written, or were there already two forms of it?

Part 8 · What the Greek has less of

The Greek is shorter

Greek Jeremiah is somewhere between one eighth and one sixth shorter than the Hebrew, depending on who measures. You can see it without any Greek.

Titles and formulas

Shorter names

Where the Hebrew says "Jeremiah the prophet" or "Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon," the Greek often has just the name.

Whole passages

Missing verses

Jeremiah 33:14 to 26, a promise about David's line and the priests, has no counterpart in the Old Greek.

Repeated wording

Fewer doublets

Phrases the Hebrew repeats elsewhere in the book are often absent from the Greek.

“MT is 20 percent longer than LXX.”

Emanuel Tov, “The Book of Jeremiah: a Work in Progress,” Bible Review 16:3 (June 2000). MT is the Hebrew text behind your English Bible. LXX is the Greek.
Three possible explanations. What evidence would decide between them?
  1. The Greek translator trimmed the text.
  2. The Hebrew he translated was already shorter.
  3. The Hebrew copy behind our Bible was expanded later.
NETS introduction to Ieremias

How the translator worked

  1. Isomorphism. The translator’s basic norm was to represent nearly every element of the Hebrew.
  2. Problems in the source text are generally passed on to the reader.

Why it matters: a translator who follows the Hebrew this closely is an unlikely trimmer. That points toward explanation 2, though it does not prove it. Pietersma and Saunders, “To the Reader of Ieremias,” NETS (2007), pp. 876–880.

Part 10 · The evidence

A Hebrew scroll that matches the Greek

4Q71, also called 4QJerb, is a Hebrew scroll from Qumran Cave 4. It dates to roughly the late third or early second century BC.

What survives on the leather
  • Parts of Jeremiah 9:22 to 10:21, in Hebrew, with verse 11 in Aramaic.
  • Where the text survives, the verse order follows the Greek.
  • Verses 10:6 to 8 and 10:10 are not there.
What scholars infer
  • That the Greek translator worked from a Hebrew text like this one, so the shortness began in Hebrew.
  • That the rest of the scroll, including the placement of the nations, also matched the Greek. That part is a reasonable assumption, not something the fragment shows.
Both forms existed at the same time. Other Qumran copies of Jeremiah (4Q70 and 4Q72) follow the longer Hebrew form. Scholars describe this as two editions of the book: a shorter one behind the Greek, and a longer one behind our Bible.

Two different kinds of change

Jeremiah shows one kind of difference. The revisers we met earlier show the other. Keeping them apart is the skill of this module.

Part 12 · Where evangelical scholarship lands

Which form is closer to the original?

Evangelical commentaries lean toward the shorter Greek reflecting an earlier Hebrew edition. They differ on what follows from that.

Kregel Exegetical Library, 2023

Michael Shepherd

Reports “the growing consensus among textual critics” that the Hebrew behind Greek Jeremiah is the earlier edition, and bases his commentary on it (p. 11).

Note on Jeremiah 25:13

NET Bible

Observes that the Greek inserts the oracles against the nations here and suggests it may represent an earlier form of the book. It does not settle which text is original.

Kerux commentary, 2022

Duane Garrett

Keeps the Greek as a translation of an earlier Egyptian edition, but rejects a slow “rolling corpus.” He says that view “reduces the book’s credibility as a faithful representation” of the prophet (p. 49, as quoted in a review).

Earlier is not the same as better. A commentary can say the Greek reflects an earlier Hebrew edition without saying it replaces the Hebrew. Shepherd goes furthest, writing his commentary on the Hebrew behind the Greek.
If the earlier edition is the shorter one, what does that change for how you read Jeremiah?

Part 13 · Revisers and Origen meet Jeremiah

What revision and Origen do to Jeremiah

Revision pulls the Greek toward the Hebrew. Jeremiah’s shortness came first, so the revisers cannot be the reason for it.

Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion

They came later

Nothing about them explains why the Old Greek of Jeremiah was short to begin with.

Origen’s asterisk

They made it longer

Hebrew-only material that the Old Greek lacked could be added to Greek copies under an asterisk.

Why it matters for Jeremiah. Later copyists often dropped the marks, so some Greek manuscripts of Jeremiah look longer than the Old Greek really was. Editors have to separate the Old Greek from these additions.

Part 14 · Follow-up exercise and questions

Edition or revision?

For each case, decide which kind of change you are looking at, then open the answer.

1. The Greek lacks Jeremiah 10:6 to 8 and 10, and a Hebrew scroll from Qumran lacks them too.

Different Hebrew edition. A Hebrew copy agrees with the Greek, so the shorter form existed before translation.

2. A Greek manuscript of Jeremiah contains a passage that Origen's marks identify as added from the Hebrew.

Later revision. Origen supplied Hebrew-only material into Greek copies. This makes the Greek longer, not shorter.

3. A Greek version follows the Hebrew so closely that the Greek reads awkwardly.

Later revision, in the style of Aquila. The Greek exists and someone re-fitted it to the Hebrew word-for-word.

4. The Oracles Against the Nations are in a different position in the Greek than in the Hebrew.

Most likely a different Hebrew edition. The arrangement is a literary one and scholars generally trace it to the Hebrew the translator used. Note that 4Q71 does not itself show this part, so it rests on inference.

Key terms

Masoretic Text (MT)The Hebrew text behind most English Bibles.
Old GreekThe earliest Greek translation, before later revisers worked on it.
EditionA distinct form of a book, with its own length and arrangement, that existed in Hebrew before it was translated.
KaigeA revising tradition that pulled the Greek toward the Hebrew, named for its habit of rendering a Hebrew word as καίγε.
RetranslationA new Greek version made from the Hebrew, such as those of Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion, as opposed to a revision of the older Greek.
HexaplaOrigen's six-column comparison of the Hebrew and Greek versions.
Asterisk and obelusOrigen's marks for material found only in the Hebrew (※) or only in the Greek (÷).
